01 — Overview

What is Generative AI?

Generative AI is a type of AI that creates new content — text, images, code, audio — instead of just putting existing content into categories. When you ask ChatGPT to write an email or Claude to explain a concept, that's generative AI at work.

Plain English: what does "generative" mean? Imagine two students. One student can look at a painting and tell you "this is Impressionism" (classifying). The other student can actually paint a new Impressionist painting from scratch (generating). Generative AI is the second student — it doesn't just recognise patterns, it creates new things that fit those patterns.

Discriminative vs Generative

Traditional AI models are discriminative — they learn to sort things into categories. A spam filter looks at an email and decides: spam or not spam? That's it. It can't write a new email; it can only judge existing ones.

A generative model does something harder: it learns the full structure of what something looks like so it can produce brand new examples. To do this, it builds a statistical picture of how the data is structured. Ask it to "write an email" and it generates one that looks like a real email — because it learned from millions of real emails what makes an email sound like an email.

Large Language Models (LLMs)

The most common form of generative AI today is the Large Language Model (LLM). An LLM is trained on enormous amounts of text — think billions of web pages, books, and articles — and learns one deceptively simple task: predict what word comes next.

That sounds almost too simple. But to consistently predict the next word, the model has to absorb grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and even common sense. The next-word prediction task is like a forcing function that makes the model learn everything about language just to do it well.

Key insight: LLMs don't "know" things like a dictionary does A dictionary stores facts as text you can look up. An LLM stores knowledge very differently — it compresses billions of examples into billions of numbers (called "weights" or "parameters"). There's no list of facts you could extract. The knowledge is smeared across all those numbers in a way that only becomes useful when you ask a question. Think of it like how a person who has read thousands of books "knows" things — they can't cite every sentence, but the knowledge shaped how they think.
02 — Core Concept

Language as Probability

An LLM doesn't decide what to say — it rolls a weighted dice. For every word it writes, it calculates the probability of each possible next word, then picks one. This section explains exactly how that works.

Plain English: what is a "probability distribution"? A probability distribution is just a list of possibilities with a percentage attached to each. Like a weather forecast: "30% chance of rain, 50% sun, 20% clouds." An LLM does the same thing for words: "42% chance the next word is 'blue', 18% chance it's 'clear', 9% chance it's 'dark'…" — across every word in its entire vocabulary (50,000+ words), every single step.

Next-token prediction

The model picks one token, adds it to the sequence, then repeats the whole process from scratch with the now-longer sequence. One step → one word → repeat until done. This repetitive loop is called autoregressive generation — "auto" because it feeds its own output back as input.

Why probability and not rules?

Early AI systems tried to handle language with hand-written rules: "if the subject is singular, the verb must be singular." This failed because natural language has infinite exceptions and edge cases. You can't write rules for every pun, idiom, slang term, regional dialect, and sarcastic remark. Probability-based models sidestep this entirely — they learn all the patterns (including exceptions) directly from millions of real examples.

Important mental model: no reasoning engine inside It feels like the model is "thinking" — but it isn't, at least not the way you might imagine. There's no reasoning engine, no fact-checker, no knowledge database being queried. It's purely: take everything written so far, calculate probabilities for every possible next word, pick one. The output feels intelligent because the probability estimation is incredibly sophisticated — but it's still just probability estimation.

How raw scores become probabilities (softmax)

Internally, the model produces a raw score (called a logit) for every word in its vocabulary. These scores aren't percentages yet — they can be any number, positive or negative. The softmax function converts them into proper probabilities that all add up to 100%:

softmax(score_i) = exp(score_i) / sum(exp(all_scores))

Example:
  Raw scores:  "blue" = 3.2,  "clear" = 2.1,  "dark" = 1.5
  After softmax: "blue" = 0.42, "clear" = 0.18, "dark" = 0.09  (sum = 1.0)

Think of softmax as a normaliser — it takes whatever raw scores the model computed and turns them into a proper probability distribution. The temperature parameter (covered in Section 9) tweaks these scores before softmax to make the model more or less adventurous with its word choices.

03 — Building Blocks

Tokenization

Before a model can work with text, it has to convert it into numbers. The first step is breaking text into tokens — small chunks that each get assigned an ID number. This is called tokenization, and it has surprising consequences.

Plain English: why can't the model just read words? Computers fundamentally work with numbers, not text. Tokenization is the step that turns text into numbers the model can process. You could tokenize letter-by-letter (26 possible values), or word-by-word (millions of possible words) — but neither extreme is efficient. Tokens are a middle ground: common short words get their own token, longer or rarer words get split into 2–3 pieces.

What is a token?

A token is roughly 3–4 characters on average in English — but it doesn't line up neatly with words. Common words ("the", "is", "of") are a single token. Longer or rarer words split into multiple tokens. Punctuation and even spaces are often their own tokens.

How the vocabulary is built: Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)

The most common tokenization algorithm is called Byte-Pair Encoding. Here's how it works in plain terms: start with every single character as its own token. Then, find the two tokens that appear next to each other most often and merge them into one new token. Repeat this thousands of times. After enough merges, common words and syllables have their own tokens, while rare words get broken into smaller pieces. The result is a vocabulary of about 50,000–100,000 tokens that efficiently covers most text.

Why tokenization matters

Context window is in tokens

When a model says "128k context window", it means 128,000 tokens — not words. A typical English page is ~500 words ≈ 650 tokens.

Counting & spelling quirks

LLMs struggle to count characters or reverse strings because they see tokens, not individual characters. "strawberry" may be ["straw", "berry"] — the model never sees each letter.

Non-English languages

Vocabularies trained mostly on English use more tokens for other languages. Chinese or Arabic text can take 2–3× as many tokens as equivalent English, eating into the context window.

API cost

LLM APIs charge per token (input + output). Knowing your token count helps estimate and control cost.

04 — Representations

Embeddings

Token IDs are just arbitrary numbers — 4521 means "king" only because we decided it does. To do anything useful, each token needs to be converted into a list of numbers that actually captures its meaning. These lists of numbers are called embeddings.

Plain English: what is an embedding? Think of it like GPS coordinates, but for meaning. Just as every location on Earth has coordinates (latitude, longitude), every word gets coordinates in "meaning space." Words that mean similar things end up near each other. "Dog" and "puppy" have similar coordinates. "Dog" and "quantum" are far apart. The model uses these coordinates to understand relationships between words. Instead of just 2 coordinates (lat/lon), embeddings use thousands of dimensions — capturing thousands of subtle aspects of meaning simultaneously.

The embedding table

Every model has an embedding matrix — essentially a giant lookup table. For each of the 50,000 tokens in the vocabulary, there's a row of numbers (typically 4,096 numbers in a large model). When the model sees token ID 4521 ("king"), it looks up row 4521 in this table and gets back 4,096 numbers. These numbers are learned during training — they start random and gradually shift to capture meaning.

vocab size: 50,000 tokens
embedding dim: 4,096 numbers per token

Embedding matrix shape: [50,000 × 4,096]
Total parameters in embedding layer: 205 million

Meaning in geometry

The magic of embeddings: semantically similar tokens end up close together in this high-dimensional space. Relationships become vector arithmetic:

Embedding space — 2D projection (real embeddings are 4096+ dimensions)

Similar words cluster together. The vector from man→king is the same as woman→queen — meaning is geometry. vec("king") − vec("man") + vec("woman") ≈ vec("queen")

This is why models can reason about analogies and relationships — the arithmetic of meaning is baked into the geometry of the embedding space.

The problem: same word, different meanings

Raw embeddings from the lookup table are static — "bank" always maps to the same coordinates, whether it means a river bank or a financial bank. That's a problem. The word "bank" in "I sat by the river bank" vs "I opened a bank account" should mean very different things to the model.

This is the Transformer's main job: take the static starting embeddings and update them based on surrounding words, so "bank" ends up with different coordinates in each sentence. After passing through the model's layers, "bank" near "river" and "water" gets different coordinates than "bank" near "account" and "money." This context-aware updating is what the attention mechanism does (explained in Section 6).

Clearing up the terminology These three words are often used interchangeably but mean slightly different things: Token = the text chunk (the word or word-piece). Token ID = the number assigned to that chunk (like a dictionary entry number). Embedding = the list of numbers that represents that token's meaning. The model's internal processing works entirely with embeddings — tokens and IDs are just the on/off-ramp.
05 — Core Architecture

The Transformer Architecture

The Transformer is the engine inside every major AI model — GPT, Claude, Gemini, LLaMA. Introduced in 2017, it revolutionised how AI processes text by letting every word look at every other word at the same time, rather than reading left-to-right one word at a time.

Plain English: what does the Transformer actually do? Imagine you're editing a document. Old AI (RNNs) read it like a person — word by word, left to right, holding a mental summary as they go. By the time they reach the end of a long document, early details are fuzzy. The Transformer is like having 10,000 eyes that all read the entire document simultaneously, letting every word directly "see" every other word at once. That's why it understands context so much better.

The big picture

A Transformer processes all tokens in a sequence at the same time (in parallel), rather than one by one. Each token's meaning gets updated by looking at what every other token says — through the attention mechanism. This updating happens in many layers stacked on top of each other: the first layers pick up simple patterns (word order, punctuation), later layers pick up complex meaning (who is doing what to whom, what emotion is being expressed).

Key components explained

Positional Encoding — teaching the model about word order

Because the Transformer reads all tokens simultaneously (not left-to-right), it doesn't naturally know which word came first. Positional encoding fixes this by adding a small position-specific signal to each token's embedding — essentially tagging each word with its position number (1st, 2nd, 3rd…). Without this, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would look identical to the model. Modern models use a technique called RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) that handles very long texts efficiently.

Residual Connections — a shortcut for deep networks

A Transformer can have 96 or more layers stacked on top of each other. Training very deep networks is notoriously hard — adjustments made at the top struggle to reach the bottom layers (called the "vanishing gradient" problem). Residual connections solve this with a simple trick: after each layer, add the original input back to the output. This creates a shortcut that allows training signals to flow directly through many layers without getting lost. Formula: output = what_layer_did(x) + x.

Layer Normalization — keeping numbers stable

As numbers flow through dozens of layers, they can grow wildly large or shrink to near zero — making the model numerically unstable and hard to train. Layer normalization is like a thermostat that rescales the numbers at each layer back to a manageable range. It doesn't change what the model "knows" — it just keeps the math well-behaved.

Feed-Forward Network (FFN) — where knowledge is stored

After the attention step, each token's updated representation passes through a small neural network independently. This two-layer network is where researchers believe the model stores its factual knowledge — specific facts, language patterns, and world knowledge get "looked up" here. The FFN is built wider than it is deep: a model with 4,096-dimensional embeddings typically expands to 16,384 inside the FFN, then compresses back to 4,096. This wide-then-narrow shape packs in a lot of capacity.

Why bigger models are smarter More layers = more rounds of refining meaning. Wider dimensions = richer representations. Bigger FFN = more knowledge stored. This is why GPT-4 (estimated ~1.8 trillion parameters) dramatically outperforms GPT-2 (1.5 billion parameters) — not because of a fundamentally different design, but sheer scale. More parameters = more capacity to learn patterns from more data.

06 — The Core Innovation

The Attention Mechanism

Attention is the single most important idea in modern AI. It's how the model figures out which words are relevant to which other words — and borrows meaning accordingly. Once you understand attention, you understand what makes LLMs so powerful.

The problem attention solves

Consider: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired." What does "it" refer to? You know immediately — the animal. But how? You connected a pronoun ("it") to a noun ("animal") that appeared 8 words earlier. This kind of long-range connection is trivial for humans but was very hard for AI before the Transformer. Attention is the mechanism that solves this: it lets the word "it" reach back across the sentence, find "animal," and borrow its meaning.

Plain English: attention as a search engine inside the model Think of attention like a library search. Every word in the sentence is both a searcher (looking for relevant information) and a document (offering information about itself). Each word asks "what context do I need to understand my meaning?" and searches the other words for relevant answers. The words with the most relevant information contribute most to updating the searcher's meaning.

Queries, Keys, and Values (Q, K, V) — the search mechanics

Each token generates three things from its embedding:

Query (Q) — "what I'm looking for"

Represents what the current token wants to find information about. Like a search query.

Key (K) — "what I advertise"

Represents what each token offers as information. Like the index key in a database.

Value (V) — "what I give"

The actual content that gets mixed into the attending token's representation if the match is strong.

How the scores are calculated

# Step 1 — score how relevant each word is (dot product = similarity measure):
score("it" asking, "animal" answering) = dot_product(Q_"it", K_"animal") / sqrt(128)

# Step 2 — convert scores to percentages (softmax so they add up to 100%):
attention_weight = softmax(all scores)
# Result: "animal" gets 61%, "tired" gets 28%, others share the rest

# Step 3 — borrow content proportionally:
new_meaning_of_"it" = 0.61 × Value_"animal" + 0.28 × Value_"tired" + ...

The / sqrt(128) scaling is a housekeeping detail — without it, the scores would grow too large in high-dimensional space and make the softmax collapse to one winner. The scaling keeps the distribution well-spread.

Multi-Head Attention — looking from multiple angles

Instead of doing this search process once, the model does it 32 times simultaneously (in a large model), each time with different learned Q/K/V projections. Each "head" independently asks a different kind of question about the text:

  • One head might focus on: "which noun does this pronoun refer to?"
  • Another head: "what is this verb's subject?"
  • Another head: "what adjectives describe this noun?"
  • Another head: "what words nearby give positional context?"

The model learns which questions are useful during training — we don't hand-program them. All 32 results get stitched back together into a single output of the same size as the input. This gives the model a rich, multi-faceted view of each word's meaning in context.

Why attention is so much better than what came before The previous approach (RNNs) read text like a person reading out loud — one word at a time, keeping a running mental summary. The problem: by the time you reach word 200, your memory of word 1 is dim. Attention has no such forgetting — every word can directly examine every other word in one step, no matter how far apart they are. A word at the start of a chapter can directly influence a word at the end. Distance is irrelevant.

Causal masking — preventing the model from "cheating"

During training, we want the model to predict the next word without already knowing what it is. This means we need to block each token from looking at future tokens. We do this by setting those attention scores to negative infinity before the softmax step — they come out as zero weight, effectively invisible. This blocking is called causal masking. At inference time (when the model is generating text), there are no future tokens anyway — the model is writing them one at a time.

07 — Learning

How Models Are Trained

Training an LLM is the process of starting with billions of random numbers and gradually nudging them — using trillions of examples — until the model is good at predicting the next word. It's like teaching by giving the model an exam over and over until it gets better. This happens in two main phases.

Phase 1: Pre-training — learning from the internet

The model starts with all its weights set to random numbers (it knows nothing). It's then shown an enormous collection of text — web pages, books, code, scientific papers — often trillions of words. For each piece of text, the model tries to predict the next word. It will be wrong constantly at first. The training process measures how wrong (the "loss"), then works backwards through the network to figure out which weights made the prediction wrong, and nudges them slightly in the right direction. This backward calculation is called backpropagation.

Simplified training loop — repeated trillions of times:
  1. Show the model: "The sky is ___"
  2. Model guesses the next word (probably wrong at first)
  3. Check: the correct answer was "blue"
  4. Measure how wrong the guess was (loss)
  5. Work backwards to find which weights caused the error (backpropagation)
  6. Nudge those weights slightly toward better answers
  7. Repeat with the next piece of text

This loop runs on thousands of specialised chips (GPUs/TPUs) for weeks or months. After all this training, the result is a base model — a model that has absorbed vast language knowledge but doesn't know how to have a helpful conversation. Ask it a question and it might just continue the text in an unexpected direction, because it was only trained to predict what comes next, not to answer questions helpfully.

Phase 2: Fine-tuning — making it a useful assistant

The base model is smart but not helpful. It needs a second phase to learn how to behave as an assistant — following instructions, being polite, refusing harmful requests. This involves showing it carefully curated examples and adjusting it based on human feedback:

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

Show the model thousands of (question, ideal answer) pairs written by humans. The model learns the format: "when given a question, respond helpfully like this." Think of it as showing the model role models to copy.

RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)

Human raters compare pairs of model responses and say which is better. A separate "reward model" learns to predict what humans prefer. The main model is then trained to maximise that score — like training with a teacher who gives grades, not corrections.

DPO / Constitutional AI

Newer, simpler alternatives to RLHF. DPO directly trains the model on "preferred vs rejected" response pairs without needing a separate reward model. Constitutional AI (Anthropic's approach) has the model critique its own responses against a set of principles.

All knowledge is in the weights

There is no separate file of facts, no database being queried. Every piece of knowledge — grammar, history, coding patterns, world facts — is encoded across billions of floating-point numbers. A "7B model" has 7 billion such numbers; a "70B model" has 70 billion. More numbers = more capacity to store patterns from more training data.

Why training costs millions but you pay pennies per query Training GPT-3 cost an estimated $5M+ in electricity and hardware. But once trained, the weights are fixed. Running the model to answer a question (called "inference") is comparatively cheap — pennies per thousand tokens. The expensive learning happens once; the cheap answering happens billions of times.
08 — Inference

How Generation Works

When you hit send on a message, the model doesn't read your prompt and then write out the full response. It writes one word at a time, re-reading everything from scratch before each word. This section explains exactly what happens between you pressing send and reading the response.

Plain English: why one word at a time? Each new word depends on all the words before it. "The cat sat on the ___" → "mat" is likely. But "The cat sat on the mat and then it jumped onto the ___" → now "table," "counter," or "bed" become more likely. The model can only know the right next word after it has already written everything that came before it. So it genuinely must go one word at a time.

What happens step by step

prompt = "The capital of France is"

Step 1: model sees ["The","capital","of","France","is"]
        → predicts next: {"Paris": 0.72, "Lyon": 0.04, ...}
        → samples "Paris"

Step 2: model sees ["The","capital","of","France","is","Paris"]
        → predicts next: {"." : 0.61, "," : 0.18, ...}
        → samples "."

Step 3: model sees full sequence including "Paris."
        → predicts next (possibly <end> token or more content)
        → stops when it generates the end-of-sequence token

The KV Cache — avoiding redundant work

If the model re-reads the entire conversation from scratch for every single new word it generates, that would be enormously wasteful. The KV Cache is an optimisation: the first time the model reads your prompt, it saves the Key and Value matrices for every token. For each subsequent new token, it only processes that one new token and pulls the saved K/V data from the cache — instead of reprocessing everything from scratch. This is why generation speed stays relatively consistent rather than slowing down dramatically as the response grows.

Why long contexts cost more and run slower Attention has to compare every word to every other word. Double the length → four times the comparisons (it scales with length²). A 128k-token context window means up to 128,000 × 128,000 = 16.4 billion comparisons per layer, per token generated. This is why a 128k-context query costs significantly more than a 4k one, and why long responses take longer to stream.

09 — Controls

Key Generation Parameters

The model calculates probabilities for every possible next word — but how it then picks from those probabilities is up to you. These parameters are the dials that let you make the model more or less creative, focused, or verbose.

Temperature — the creativity dial

Temperature controls how adventurous the model is when picking the next word. Low temperature makes it conservative (picks the most likely word almost every time). High temperature makes it bolder (gives unusual words a real chance). It works by scaling the raw scores before converting to probabilities:

# Before softmax, divide logits by temperature T:
adjusted_logit_i = logit_i / T

Low T  (0.1) → distribution becomes very peaked (model is "confident")
             → almost always picks the highest-probability token
             → outputs are deterministic and repetitive

High T (1.5) → distribution flattens out
             → lower-probability tokens get more chance
             → outputs are more diverse, surprising, sometimes incoherent

T = 1.0      → unchanged distribution (model's raw probabilities)

Context Window — the model's "working memory"

The context window is the total amount of text the model can see at one time — your messages, the system prompt, the model's own replies, all of it. Think of it as the model's working memory: anything inside the window is available; anything outside is completely gone. The model cannot remember past sessions, retrieve old conversations, or look back further than the window allows.

Everything inside the window is visible. Everything outside is completely invisible.

If it's not in the window, the model doesn't know it exists.

What counts toward the limit

System prompt + conversation history + user message + model's own output so far. All of these consume context window tokens.

What happens at the limit

The model either refuses to continue, truncates the oldest content, or degrades in quality — it literally cannot "see" what has been pushed out.

Typical sizes (2025–2026)

GPT-4o: 128k. Claude 3.5 Sonnet: 200k. Gemini 1.5 Pro: 1M. Larger ≠ always better — long-context retrieval quality degrades.

"Lost in the middle" problem

Models recall information near the start and end of long contexts better than the middle. Important facts should not be buried in the middle of a massive prompt.

Top-k Sampling — cut off the long tail

The model's vocabulary has 50,000+ words. Most of them have near-zero probability in any given context. Top-k sampling says: only consider the k most likely words, throw out everything else, then sample from those k. This prevents the model from ever picking a completely absurd word just by statistical fluke.

Top-p (Nucleus) Sampling — a smarter version of top-k

Top-k always keeps exactly k words regardless of context. Top-p is smarter: it keeps however many words are needed to cover p% of the total probability. When the model is confident, one word covers 95% of the probability — top-p picks just that one. When the model is uncertain, you might need 80 words to cover 90% — top-p picks all 80. This adapts naturally to the model's confidence level.

Max Tokens (Max New Tokens)

A hard cap on how many tokens the model will generate in a single response, regardless of whether it's finished. Set this to avoid runaway generation and control API costs. It does not affect response quality — the model simply stops when the cap is reached, even mid-sentence.

Stop Sequences

One or more strings that, if generated, cause the model to stop immediately. Useful for structured output (e.g., stop at }\n after JSON generation) or conversation turns (stop at Human:).

Encoder-only — the "reader" (e.g. BERT, RoBERTa)

This design reads the entire input all at once, with every word seeing every other word simultaneously (bidirectional). It produces a deep understanding of the full text. The catch: it doesn't generate — it can't write new text, only understand existing text. Use it for tasks that require understanding: is this review positive? What's the main topic of this document? Which words are people's names?

Bidirectional attention

Each token sees the full context — both left and right — giving excellent representations for understanding tasks.

Best for

Classification, NER, sentiment analysis, sentence similarity, search embeddings. Cannot generate text.

Decoder-only — the "writer" (e.g. GPT, Claude, LLaMA, Gemini)

This is the architecture behind every major chat AI. It generates text left-to-right, with each word only able to see the words that came before it (causal masking). It's trained to predict the next word. Turns out: if you scale this design up enough and train it on enough text, it becomes remarkably capable at conversation, coding, reasoning, and much more. This is the dominant architecture for modern AI assistants.

Causal attention

Each token attends only to the past. Natural for generation — the model predicts the future one token at a time.

Best for

Text generation, chat, code generation, summarisation, Q&A, instruction following. Nearly all frontier models use this.

Encoder-Decoder — the "translator" (e.g. T5, BART)

This combines both: an encoder reads the source text fully (bidirectionally), then a decoder generates the output while being able to look back at the encoder's full understanding. This two-stage design makes it natural for tasks where the input and output are structurally separate — like translating English to French, or summarising a document into bullet points.

Cross-attention

Decoder's Q queries the encoder's K/V. Decoder tokens can "look at" the full encoded input at every generation step.

Best for

Translation (input language → output language), summarisation of fixed documents. Less common for chat at frontier scale.

11 — Limitations

Hallucinations & Limits

LLMs will confidently tell you things that are completely wrong. This isn't a bug that will eventually be fixed — it's a direct consequence of how the model works. Understanding why helps you use AI tools safely.

Plain English: why does the model make things up? The model was trained to produce text that looks plausible — not text that is factually correct. It has no internal fact-checker, no ability to "look something up," and no way to distinguish between "I'm confident about this" and "I'm making this up." When asked about something it doesn't have reliable information about, it does exactly what it always does: finds the most statistically plausible-sounding continuation. Unfortunately, "plausible-sounding" and "factually correct" are not the same thing.

Why hallucinations happen

During training, the model was rewarded for predicting text well — not for being accurate. It learned to produce responses that sound like a knowledgeable person wrote them, because that's what appeared in the training data. When queried about something rare or uncertain, it produces text that sounds like an authoritative answer, regardless of whether the content is true. There's no "I don't know" reflex — just the next most plausible word.

Common failure modes

Factual confabulation

Inventing plausible-sounding but false facts: wrong dates, non-existent citations, incorrect statistics.

Knowledge cutoff

Training data has a cutoff date. The model has no knowledge of events after that date and may confabulate about them.

Long-tail knowledge

Obscure facts seen rarely in training data are more likely to be distorted or wrong. Popular topics are more reliable.

Sycophancy

Models trained on human feedback learn to agree with the user. If you push back on a correct answer, the model may capitulate — even when it was right.

Other fundamental limitations

  • No persistent memory: Every new conversation starts completely blank. The model has no recollection of what you talked about yesterday, last hour, or even two minutes ago in a different tab. It only knows what's in the current context window.
  • No real-time information: The model's knowledge was frozen when training ended. It has no internet access and cannot look up current news, prices, sports scores, or anything else (unless given tools to do so).
  • Different output each time: With temperature > 0, the same prompt can produce different answers on different runs. This is by design (sampling from a distribution), but it means the model isn't like a calculator — you can't rely on it to give the identical answer twice.
  • Context-window amnesia: Once content scrolls out of the context window, it's completely inaccessible. The model can't "remember" it or retrieve it — it simply doesn't exist as far as the model is concerned.
  • Weak at exact arithmetic and counting: The model processes tokens, not numbers. "153 × 247" gets broken into tokens like "153", "×", "247" — the model learned patterns about how arithmetic answers look, not actual calculation. It's surprisingly good at rough estimates but unreliable for exact multi-digit arithmetic.
  • Prompt sensitivity: Tiny changes in how you phrase a question can change the answer significantly. This is very different from normal software, where the same input always produces the same output. Models are statistically powerful but brittle in unexpected ways.

Mitigations

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

Retrieve relevant documents and inject them into the context. The model grounds its answer in provided evidence rather than relying solely on weights.

Tool use / function calling

Give the model access to a calculator, search engine, or database. Offload exact computation to deterministic tools.

Structured output + validation

Force JSON output with a schema and validate it programmatically. Don't trust free-form outputs for critical data.

Temperature = 0 + citations

Low temperature reduces variance. Requiring citations forces the model to surface evidence, making hallucinations easier to detect.

The right mental model Think of an LLM as an extremely well-read, articulate intern who has no access to the internet, cannot verify their own memories, sometimes misremembers things confidently, and has never lived through any event more recent than their training cutoff. Useful — but verify anything important.
