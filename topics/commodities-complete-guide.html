Section 01

Foundations of Commodities

1.1 What Is a Commodity?

A commodity is a basic, standardized, interchangeable good used in commerce. Unlike manufactured products, one unit of a commodity is essentially identical to another — a barrel of WTI crude from Texas is fungible with another barrel of the same grade. This standardization is what makes commodities tradeable on organized exchanges.

Commodities are the raw materials that underpin the global economy: the oil that fuels transportation, the copper that wires cities, the wheat that feeds populations. They sit at the intersection of physical supply chains and financial markets, creating a uniquely complex asset class.

1.2 Classification of Commodities

Figure 1.1 — Commodity classification taxonomy showing the four major categories and their sub-types.

1.3 Key Characteristics

Standardization

Exchange-traded commodities must meet precise quality specifications. WTI crude oil, for instance, is defined as light sweet crude with an API gravity of 37–42° and sulfur content below 0.42%. This standardization means any buyer receiving delivery knows exactly what they're getting, enabling price transparency and liquidity.

Seasonality

Many commodities exhibit cyclical price patterns. Natural gas prices typically spike in winter (heating demand) and summer (cooling demand). Agricultural commodities follow planting and harvest cycles — U.S. corn prices often weaken around October harvest, then strengthen through winter. Understanding these patterns is essential for timing trades and managing risk.

Storage and Transportation Constraints

Unlike financial assets, commodities are physical. Storing crude oil requires tank farms; natural gas needs underground caverns or LNG facilities; grains require silos with temperature control. These real-world constraints create storage costs that directly affect pricing. Landlocked commodities face additional transport economics — WTI's delivery point at Cushing, Oklahoma, creates unique logistics that can cause its price to diverge from seaborne Brent crude.

1.4 Spot vs. Futures Pricing

The spot price is the current price for immediate delivery of a commodity. The futures price is the agreed-upon price for delivery at a specified future date. The relationship between these two prices reveals critical market information.

Figure 1.2 — Contango occurs when futures prices exceed the spot price (upward-sloping curve), while backwardation occurs when futures trade below spot (downward-sloping). These concepts are explored deeply in Section 6.

Key Relationship: Futures Price ≈ Spot Price + Storage Costs + Financing Costs − Convenience Yield. When holders of a commodity gain value from physical possession (e.g., refiners needing crude to keep running), the convenience yield can push futures below spot, creating backwardation.

1.5 Supply and Demand Fundamentals

Figure 1.3 — When demand shifts right (D → D'), the equilibrium price rises. In commodities, demand shifts can be driven by economic growth, weather events, or geopolitical disruptions. Supply shifts (not shown) work symmetrically — an OPEC production cut shifts supply left, raising prices.

Section 02

Structure of Commodities Markets

2.1 How Different Commodity Markets Work

Oil Markets: Brent vs. WTI

The global oil market revolves around two primary benchmarks. Brent Crude is the international benchmark, representing oil from the North Sea, priced for delivery at Sullom Voe terminal in Scotland. It serves as the reference price for roughly two-thirds of globally traded crude. WTI (West Texas Intermediate) is the North American benchmark, delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma — a landlocked hub connected by pipelines. The Brent-WTI spread reflects relative supply-demand dynamics: when U.S. production surges faster than pipeline capacity (as happened during the 2011–2014 shale boom), WTI can trade at significant discounts to Brent.

Precious Metals: Gold and Silver

Gold operates as both a commodity and a monetary asset. The London Bullion Market Association (LBMA) sets the twice-daily London Gold Fix, the global benchmark for physical gold. COMEX (part of CME Group) handles the majority of gold futures trading. Gold prices are driven by a complex mix of jewelry demand (primarily India and China), central bank purchases, investment flows (ETFs like GLD), and its role as an inflation/crisis hedge.

Agricultural Markets

Agricultural commodities are uniquely affected by weather, government policy (subsidies, tariffs, biofuel mandates), and biological constraints (crop yields, disease). The U.S. remains the world's largest producer and exporter of corn and soybeans, making USDA crop reports market-moving events. Coffee markets are split between Arabica (traded on ICE, priced in cents/lb) and Robusta (traded on ICE London, priced in USD/tonne).

Section 04

Derivative Products in Commodities

4.1 Futures

A futures contract is a standardized, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell a specific quantity of a commodity at a predetermined price on a future date. Futures are the backbone of commodity markets.

Figure 4.1 — Futures payoff is linear and symmetric. A long position profits dollar-for-dollar when price rises above the entry price F₀; a short position profits when price falls. Both have unlimited upside and downside.

Real-World Example

Hedging Jet Fuel Costs: An airline expects to consume 10 million gallons of jet fuel in Q4. Current price: $2.50/gallon. They buy NYMEX RBOB Gasoline futures (a common proxy) to lock in their cost. If jet fuel rises to $3.00/gallon, the $0.50/gallon loss on physical purchases is offset by a $0.50/gallon gain on futures — total fuel cost remains approximately $2.50/gallon plus basis risk.

4.2 Forwards

A forward contract is similar to a futures contract but is privately negotiated (OTC), customizable in size/delivery terms, and not marked to market daily. Forwards carry counterparty credit risk since there is no clearinghouse guarantee.

Futures vs. Forwards: Futures are standardized, exchange-traded, margined daily, and nearly credit-risk-free. Forwards are bespoke, OTC, settled at maturity, and carry counterparty risk. A gold mining company might use an OTC forward to lock in a specific delivery schedule at a specific refinery — something impossible with a standard exchange contract.

4.3 Options (Calls and Puts)

Options give the holder the right but not the obligation to buy (call) or sell (put) a commodity at a specified strike price by a certain date. The buyer pays a premium upfront; their maximum loss is limited to this premium.

Figure 4.2 — Long call: pay premium, unlimited upside if price exceeds strike + premium. Long put: pay premium, profit if price falls below strike − premium. The asymmetric payoff is the key advantage of options over futures for hedging.

Real-World Example

Producer Floor Protection: A gold miner buys put options at a $1,800/oz strike, paying a $50/oz premium. If gold drops to $1,600/oz, the miner exercises the put, selling at $1,800 — net effective price $1,750/oz ($1,800 − $50 premium). If gold rises to $2,200/oz, the miner lets the put expire worthless, sells at market, and net receives $2,150/oz ($2,200 − $50 premium). The put acts as a price floor.

4.4 Swaps

A commodity swap is an OTC agreement where two parties exchange cash flows based on commodity prices. The most common is a fixed-for-floating swap: one party pays a fixed price per unit, the other pays the prevailing market (floating) price. At each settlement date, only the net difference changes hands.

Figure 4.3 — In a fixed-for-floating commodity swap, the consumer achieves cost certainty while the bank manages the floating-price risk through its own hedging operations or portfolio offsets.

4.5 Swaptions

A swaption gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to enter into a commodity swap at a future date at predetermined terms. It combines the flexibility of options with the cash-flow structure of swaps. A refinery might buy a swaption granting the right to enter into a crude oil swap at $80/bbl starting in 6 months — if oil prices have risen above that level, they exercise the swaption; if not, they walk away and buy at lower market prices.

Section 06

Pricing and Curves

6.1 Contango and Backwardation

Contango occurs when futures prices are higher than the spot price, creating an upward-sloping forward curve. This is the "normal" state for many commodities, reflecting the cost of storage, insurance, and financing required to hold inventory. Backwardation occurs when futures trade below spot — typically during supply shortages when the convenience yield (the benefit of holding physical inventory) exceeds storage costs.

Figure 6.1 — The shape of the futures curve has direct P&L implications for investors who must "roll" expiring contracts into the next month. Persistent contango erodes returns; persistent backwardation enhances them.

6.2 Cost of Carry Model

F(t,T) = S(t) × e(r + u − y)(T − t)

Where F is the futures price, S is the spot price, r is the risk-free rate, u is the storage cost rate, y is the convenience yield, and (T−t) is the time to maturity. When r + u > y, the market is in contango. When y > r + u, the market is in backwardation.

6.3 Convenience Yield

The convenience yield is the implied benefit of holding physical inventory rather than a futures position. A refinery with crude in tanks can keep running during a supply disruption; a futures contract cannot do that. Convenience yield rises during scarcity (tight inventories) and falls during glut conditions. It is not directly observable — it is backed out from the relationship between spot and futures prices using the cost-of-carry formula.

Value at Risk (VaR)

VaR estimates the maximum expected loss over a given time horizon at a specified confidence level. A 1-day 95% VaR of $2 million means there is a 5% chance of losing more than $2 million in a single day. For commodities, VaR models must account for fat-tailed distributions (prices can spike 10%+ in a day on geopolitical events), seasonality, and the mean-reverting tendencies of many commodity prices.

7.2 Commodity-Specific Risks

Basis Risk

The risk that the hedge instrument and the physical exposure don't move in perfect lockstep. Example: An airline hedges jet fuel with NYMEX RBOB gasoline futures. The jet fuel "crack spread" vs. RBOB can widen unpredictably, leaving the airline partially unhedged. Basis risk is unavoidable when exact commodity-grade futures don't exist.

Liquidity Risk

Some commodity markets are thin, especially for far-dated contracts or exotic grades. An attempt to exit a large position in illiquid back-month natural gas futures might move the market 5–10 ticks against you. Liquidity can evaporate entirely during crises — as seen when WTI went negative in April 2020.

Geopolitical Risk

Commodities are inherently exposed to geopolitics. OPEC production decisions, U.S. sanctions on Iranian or Russian oil, Strait of Hormuz disruptions, export bans on agricultural products — these events can cause sudden, large price dislocations that overwhelm quantitative risk models.

Regulatory & Delivery Risk

Position limits imposed by the CFTC can constrain trading strategies. Delivery-related risks include storage capacity constraints (Cushing, Oklahoma), quality disputes, and force majeure events. Environmental regulations (e.g., carbon taxes, sulfur limits) create long-term structural risks.

7.3 Hedging Strategies

Figure 7.1 — Hedging strategies compared. Futures hedge locks in a fixed effective cost (flat line). A put option creates a floor but allows participation in favourable moves. A collar (buy put, sell call) caps upside but reduces premium cost. The unhedged position follows the market directly.

Hedging Example — Airline Fuel Costs

Scenario: An airline expects to consume 500 million gallons of jet fuel over the next year. Current market: $2.50/gallon.

Strategy 1 — Futures: Buy NYMEX heating oil futures to lock in $2.50/gallon. Result: fixed cost of $1.25 billion regardless of market moves. Trade-off: no benefit if prices fall.

Strategy 2 — Put Option: Buy $2.50 puts at $0.10/gallon premium. Cost: $50 million. If prices rise to $3.00, effective cost = $2.60/gallon ($2.50 floor + premium). If prices fall to $2.00, let puts expire; effective cost = $2.10 ($2.00 market + $0.10 premium).

Strategy 3 — Collar: Buy $2.50 put, sell $2.80 call. Premium cost: near zero (call premium offsets put premium). Effective cost range: $2.50 – $2.80/gallon.

9.2 Index Construction and Rolling

Commodity indices track fully collateralized futures positions. Since futures contracts expire, the index must periodically "roll" — selling the expiring near-month contract and buying the next-month contract. This mechanical process has profound return implications.

9.3 Components of Commodity Index Returns

Figure 9.1 — Investors in commodity index funds receive three return components. In contango markets, the roll yield is negative, which can significantly erode total returns even if spot prices are flat or rising. The collateral return tracks short-term interest rates.

Roll Yield Impact: Between 2005 and 2020, the S&P GSCI Spot Index and the Total Return Index frequently diverged by 5–10% annually, with the Total Return version underperforming — almost entirely due to negative roll yield from persistent contango in energy futures. This is the "hidden cost" of passive commodity investing.

10.2 Gold as an Inflation Hedge

Gold has historically served as a store of value during inflationary periods and currency debasement. During 2020–2022, as U.S. CPI inflation surged from ~1.4% to over 9%, gold initially rallied from $1,500 to $2,050/oz (March 2022). However, the relationship is not straightforward — when the Fed aggressively raised rates, gold declined despite high inflation because rising real yields increased the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset.

Key Insight: Gold is more accurately a real interest rate hedge than a pure inflation hedge. Gold tends to perform best when real rates (nominal rates minus inflation) are negative or falling, regardless of the absolute inflation level.

10.3 Portfolio Allocation Strategies

Strategic Allocation (5–15%)

A permanent allocation to commodities for diversification and inflation protection. Typically implemented via broad commodity index fund (BCOM or GSCI). Rebalanced quarterly or annually. Benefits from low correlation with equities and bonds over long periods.

Tactical Allocation

Active over/underweighting based on macro views. Increase commodity exposure when: inflation expectations rise, USD weakens, global PMIs expand, inventory levels are low. Reduce when: rate hikes accelerate, global demand slows, contango is steep.

Advanced Topics

11.1 Algorithmic Trading in Commodities

Algorithmic and systematic trading account for an increasing share of commodity market volumes. Key strategies include trend-following (Commodity Trading Advisors / CTAs), mean-reversion (exploiting temporary deviations from fundamental value), statistical arbitrage (cross-commodity spread trading), and calendar spread algorithms (exploiting term structure anomalies).

Commodity-specific challenges for algo design include low-frequency data in physical markets, geopolitical event risk that breaks statistical models, limit-up/limit-down restrictions on exchanges, and the need to incorporate physical fundamentals (inventory data, weather, shipping routes) alongside price data.

Continuing Your Journey

This guide has covered the full spectrum of commodity market knowledge — from basic classification and supply-demand dynamics through exchange infrastructure, derivative instruments, pricing theory, risk management, and advanced system architecture. The commodity markets are among the most complex and dynamic in finance, sitting at the intersection of physical supply chains, geopolitics, weather, and financial engineering.

To deepen your expertise, focus on building practical skills: analyze real futures curves on Bloomberg or free sources like CME Group's website, paper-trade commodity futures to understand margin mechanics, study CFTC Commitment of Traders reports to understand positioning, and follow key fundamental data releases (EIA weekly petroleum status, USDA WASDE reports, LME warehouse stocks). The theoretical knowledge in this guide becomes powerful only when combined with hands-on market experience.
